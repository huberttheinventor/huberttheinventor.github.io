PageRank is an eigenvector.

Click random links forever, and the share of time you spend on each page stops changing. Freeze those six settled shares into a column, multiply by the page-to-page link matrix once more, and the same column comes straight back — which is what makes it an eigenvector. Sorted biggest first, that column was the first score Google ever gave every page.

The whole mental model, one picture

Six pages, joined by links, and one bored random clicker

The whole mental model, one picture. Everything after it is annotation.

Nº001 · Twenty-four million pages, no time to read any of them

Score every page by imagining one clicker who never stops

The first Google held at least twenty-four million pages, far too many to rank by hand or by reading. Brin and Page's fix was a thought experiment: imagine a clicker who starts anywhere and follows links at random, forever. The share of time that clicker spends on a page, in the long run, is the page's score. Six pages are enough to run the whole idea by hand.

Source

The random surfer is defined in Brin & Page 1998, §2.1.2: a surfer who "keeps clicking on links... but eventually gets bored and starts on another random page."

Nº002 · Split evenly, but a busy page pours more

Every round, each page hands its sand to whatever it links to

Pour sand on every page as a picture of the clicker's time. Each round, a page's sand flows out along its own links, split evenly among them. A page with three out-links keeps a third of its sand on each; a page with one keeps it all on that one. A link from a page that is currently holding a lot of sand therefore pours more than the same link from a page holding little — a busy page's vote counts for more than a quiet page's.

Nº003 · Two pages that only ever talk to each other

E and F trap the sand, and every other page drains to zero

Run the rounds with no fix and watch pages A, B, C and D empty out: every page that eventually points only at E or F loses its sand for good, because E and F's only out-links point at each other. After 400 rounds with links alone, A, B, C and D all read 0, while E holds 0.467 and F holds 0.533 — the two of them keeping the clicker's entire attention, and nothing else.

Why it matters

A pair like this is called a rank sink: sand flows in and never leaves. Left alone, it breaks the whole score, because most of the web reads exactly zero.

Nº004 · The boredom rule, folded into one table

15% of the time, the clicker ignores the links and jumps anywhere

Brin and Page's actual fix: with probability d = 0.85 the clicker follows a link as usual; the rest of the time — 1−d = 15% — it gets bored and jumps to any of the six pages at random. That trickle is enough to leak every trap. Folding the jump into the link table gives the link matrix: one column per page, showing exactly where that page's sand goes each round, jump included.

Page C has three out-links, to A, B and D, so its column sends 0.308 to each of them (0.85/3 + 0.15/6). Every cell carries the 0.025 jump floor (0.15/6) even where no link exists.

Nº005 · The column the matrix hands back unchanged

That column is an eigenvector, and sorted it is the first score

Run the damped rounds and the six shares stop moving: within 0.0005 of their final value after 29 rounds, from a flat 1/6 start. Freeze them and they read A 0.109, B 0.109, C 0.230, D 0.090, E 0.236, F 0.225 — a probability distribution, since the six numbers add to 1.000. Multiply that column by the link matrix above one more time and the same six numbers come straight back. A column a matrix only stretches, never rotates, is called an eigenvector; here the stretch is exactly 1, which is why one more multiply changes nothing.

Sorted biggest first — E, C, F, A, B, D — that was the first score Google gave every page: how often the random clicker lands on it. The trap pair does not sweep the top: E leads at 0.236, but hub C is second at 0.230, ahead of F at 0.225. E and F still rank high because a page whose only out-link is to its own partner keeps the clicker the longest — the 15% jump is the only way out for either of them — but the jump also gives every other page, C included, enough of a trickle to compete. This score was one input Google combined with the words on a page (§4.5.1); it was never the whole ranking, and whether Google still uses it today is unverified and not claimed here.
