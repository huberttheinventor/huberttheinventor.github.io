Ten servers, one door.

One machine takes ten thousand requests a second and falls over, and there is no bigger machine to buy. So you buy ten small ones, and now you have a new question, asked ten thousand times a second: which of the ten gets this one? The thing that answers it is a load balancer, and it never made a single server stronger. It makes a hundred of them behave like one, which also means it can make all hundred vanish at once.

The whole mental model, one picture. Everything after it is annotation.

Take it with you: the doorman schematic, all four rules and what each one reads, on one page.

Taking turns is fair, and it is stupid.

The obvious rule is everyone in turn. Round and round: machine one, machine two, and back to the top. It is fair in the only sense a stopwatch can measure, because over a long enough run every machine gets the same number of requests. The trouble is that requests are not the same size as each other. One is a login and takes four milliseconds. The next is a forty-second report. Taking turns cannot tell those apart, because taking turns reads nothing at all before it answers, so it will happily hand the next request to a machine that is already halfway through the report. Fairness in the count is not fairness in the load.

The right answer costs you ten reads.

So read the machines first, and send the request to whichever has the fewest connections open. That is the correct answer, and you can prove it is correct, which is why it is the rule most people reach for. What it does not advertise is the price. The door now has to know the state of all ten machines at the moment it decides, which means asking all ten, or keeping a table that it keeps fresh by asking all ten. At ten machines that is invisible. At a thousand machines the door spends its time doing bookkeeping about the work instead of handing the work out, and the thing you built to remove a bottleneck has become one.

Two random looks, and you are nearly done.

Here is the part that sounds wrong. Pick two machines at random, read only those two, and send the request to the emptier of the pair. Not the emptiest of ten: the emptier of two, chosen blind. What you are left with is not a little worse than reading everything, it is almost indistinguishable from it. Mitzenmacher's 1996 result is the reason. With one random pick the busiest machine runs about log n ahead of the average; with two it runs about log log n ahead, which for any fleet you will ever own is a small number that barely moves as the fleet grows. Going to three picks buys almost nothing on top of that. The whole trick is the step from one look to two, and NGINX ships it, so your traffic has probably been through it today.

A machine that stopped is not a machine that is busy.

Every rule above is about load, and load is the wrong question if the machine is dead. So the door also pokes all ten every few seconds and asks for a sign of life, on its own timer, separately from any request. A machine that stops answering comes out of the rotation quietly, before anybody sees a failure, and goes back in when it starts answering again. This is a different axis from everything else on this page. It is not about who is emptiest, it is about who is there, and a door with a perfect balancing rule and no health checks will confidently route a tenth of your traffic into a hole.

GitHub, and the door as the thing that fails.

On the 17th of August 2026, GitHub was unreachable for seven hours and forty-seven minutes, and the servers behind the doors were fine the whole time. The doors themselves ran out of network, and the autoscaler that should have noticed was watching a different number. Then the failure fed itself: every client that got no answer asked again, and the retries arrived as ten times the normal traffic, aimed at a door that was already jammed. That is the shape of the risk you take on when you put one thing in front of a hundred things. A load balancer never made a server stronger. It makes a hundred of them behave like one, and one is a number that can go to zero.
