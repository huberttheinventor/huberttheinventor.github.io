The play button was never the hard part.

Netflix is two machines wearing one coat. One answers questions about you. The other moves several gigabytes across an ocean before you finish reading this sentence.

The whole system, one picture. Everything after it is annotation.

Take it with you: the map as an image · as a vector

Nº001

The original file never reaches you

A feature film in mastering quality is enormous — far larger than anything you would ever stream. Those masters sit in bulk object storage and are never what gets sent to a television.

What ships instead is a set of renditions: the same film encoded many times over, at different resolutions and bitrates, with different codecs for different devices. The library you browse is not a library of films. It is a library of encodes.

Nº002

The ladder is computed, not chosen

An animated short and a grain-heavy war film should not be encoded to the same bitrates. A simple image compresses beautifully; a noisy one does not. Encoding both at a fixed ladder wastes bandwidth on one and starves the other.

So the ladder is derived from the title's own complexity. Netflix published this approach as per-title encode optimisation, and later refined it further. It is the reason any table claiming fixed bitrates per resolution is describing something the industry deliberately moved away from.

Nº003

The largest lever in the whole system

If every stream in Europe were served from a datacentre in the United States, every byte would cross an ocean. That is slow, and at scale it is punishing — you would pay transit on the same popular film millions of times over.

So copies are pushed outward before anyone asks for them, onto caching servers close to viewers and often inside the viewer's own internet provider. Netflix built its own network for this: Open Connect. Watching from Paris usually means streaming from Paris.

Everything else in this map optimises bytes. This one removes the distance they travel, which is why it matters more than the rest combined.

Nº004

Small messages go one way, enormous files go another

Press play and two systems wake up. The application handles everything about you: identity, subscription, recommendations, where you left off, which rendition you should receive. Then it steps out of the way.

The video does not flow through those servers. It streams from the nearest cache. Routing petabytes of video through the same service that renders your homepage would be a bottleneck of spectacular proportions.

Once you see this split, the architecture stops looking complicated. It is a control plane and a data plane, and almost every large media system converges on the same shape eventually.

Nº005

Why quality can change mid-scene

Video is not delivered as one long stream. It is cut into short segments — a handful of seconds each — and every segment exists at every rung of the ladder.

That is what makes switching possible. The player is not committed to a quality when it presses play; it makes a fresh decision for every segment. Bandwidth drops mid-scene, the next segment arrives smaller, and playback never pauses. You experience it as "it went blurry for a second" rather than as a spinner, which is entirely the point.

Nº006

Not all outages are equal

A hit series drops and millions press play within the same hour. No single database survives that, so the application is split into many services — accounts, billing, recommendations, playback, search — each scaling on its own and, more importantly, each failing on its own.

That separation buys graded failure. Losing your watch history is an annoyance. Losing recommendations is a worse evening. Losing playback is the product being down. Ranking those, then protecting them differently, is most of what reliability engineering means here.
