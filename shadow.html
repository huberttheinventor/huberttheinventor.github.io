The dot product is a shadow.

A language model has never read a word. Inside it, every word is an arrow, and the only thing the machine ever does with two arrows is measure the shadow one throws on the other. Long shadow, they agree. No shadow, nothing in common. Shadow falling behind, they oppose. That length is the dot product, and the whole of attention is that one measurement done billions of times a second.

The whole mental model, one picture. Everything after it is annotation.

Take it with you: the shadow card, the three shadows with a word pair each, and the one line the reel left out.

A shadow is a length, and a length is a number.

Stand an arrow on a floor, put a light straight overhead, and look at what lands. The shadow is the part of the arrow that runs along the floor; the part that stands up throws nothing. Now make the floor a second arrow. The shadow the first arrow throws on the second is a length, and that length is the dot product of the two: the same number the formula a · b = |a| |b| cos θ gives you, read straight off the picture. In every picture on this page the arrow being shadowed on is drawn at unit length, so the shadow is the number itself with nothing to multiply. Make that reference arrow twice as long and the same shadow counts double, which is the |b| in the formula and the reason the film never lets it change.

Turn the arrow and the number tells you the angle.

Keep the length fixed and rotate. Pointing the same way as the reference, the shadow covers it end to end: the dot product is as large as it can be. Turn towards sideways and the shadow shortens, until at a right angle nothing lands at all and the number is zero. Keep turning and the shadow reappears behind the base, on the wrong side of the pivot, and the number goes negative; pointing straight back, it is as negative as it can be. That is cos θ doing its work, from plus one through zero to minus one, and it is why one number is enough to say how much two arrows agree. The sign says whether they agree or oppose, and the size says how strongly.

Every word is an arrow, and the machine only ever measures shadows.

A language model holds no dictionary. Each word it knows is stored as an arrow in a space with a few thousand directions, placed during training so that words used in similar ways point in similar directions. To ask whether king is like queen, the model throws one shadow on the other and reads the length. Long: alike. Near zero, as with banana: unrelated. In practice it divides that shadow by both arrows' lengths first, which is the cosine of the angle between them, so a word cannot look important merely by having a long arrow. Nothing has been looked up. The meaning of king, as far as the machine is concerned, is the set of shadows it throws on every other word.

Attention is one shadow, done to death.

The word in every headline is the same measurement, made at scale. For each word in a sentence the model builds a query arrow, the question that word is asking, and for every word a key arrow, the answer it offers. Attention throws each query's shadow onto every key at once, so one word gets a number against every other word in the sentence. The long shadows are the words it listens to; the slivers it ignores. Those numbers are squashed so they add up to one, and the word's new meaning is built from the words it listened to, in those proportions. A model with billions of parameters does this at every layer, for every word, on every pass, which is what "billions of them a second" means. It is one shadow, over and over, and it turns out that is enough.
