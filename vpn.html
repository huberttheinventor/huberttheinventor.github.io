VPN / THE VPN CARD

The VPN card: what HTTPS hides, what it doesn't, and what a VPN changes

HTTPS seals the letter. It does not seal the address on the outside of the envelope. A VPN puts the whole sealed envelope inside a second envelope, so the watcher changes: it stops being your Wi-Fi or your internet provider and becomes your VPN provider.

Who can see what

Content of the page (the letter)Which site you visit (the address)
Your Wi-Fi and your internet provider, no VPNNo, HTTPS seals itYes: the destination IP address, plus the site name when your device looks it up in plain DNS or names it in the TLS handshake
Your Wi-Fi and your internet provider, with a VPNNoNo, they only see the VPN server's address (and when, and how much)
Your VPN providerNo, if the site uses HTTPSYes, every address you visit through it
The websiteYes, it is the one you are talking toIt sees the VPN's address instead of yours, but not your login: if you are signed in, it still knows it is you

1. The site name can leak before HTTPS starts: DNS

Before your browser can connect, your device asks a DNS server for the site's address. Classic DNS is not encrypted, so anyone on the path can read the names you look up. RFC 9076 (DNS privacy considerations, section 5.1): "the HTTP traffic will be encrypted, but the DNS exchange prior to it will not be."

Fix: encrypted DNS, called DNS over HTTPS (RFC 8484, section 8.1: "DoH encrypts DNS traffic and requires authentication of the server").

  • Chrome: Settings > Privacy and security > Security > Use secure DNS (Google's help page: Chrome encrypts the lookup when it is on; in automatic mode it can fall back to an unencrypted lookup if the secure one fails).
  • Firefox: Settings > Privacy & Security > DNS over HTTPS (Advanced settings). Menus change between versions.
  • Catch: the lookup now travels to the DNS provider you pick, so that provider sees it instead of your network.

2. The TLS handshake can leak it too: SNI and ECH

Many websites share one server address, so your browser names the site it wants in the first message of the TLS handshake, the Server Name Indication (RFC 6066, section 3: it exists to reach "virtual" servers at a single underlying network address). That first message is sent before any encryption is set up, so the name is readable. RFC 9849 calls it "perhaps the most sensitive information left unencrypted in TLS 1.3".

Fix: Encrypted Client Hello (ECH, RFC 9849, an Internet Standards Track document, March 2026). The client encrypts that first message. It works only when the site has published an ECH configuration (RFC 9849, section 3.2: domains that want ECH must publish it) and your browser supports it. Many sites do not yet, so do not assume it.

3. A VPN hides the destination from your network, not from the VPN provider

Your device wraps the sealed envelope, address and all, inside a new one addressed to the VPN server (a tunnel, in the sense of RFC 4301 section 4.1: the original traffic travels inside an IP tunnel). Your Wi-Fi and internet provider see only the VPN server's address. The VPN server opens the outer layer and forwards your request, so it sees every address you visit. Before you pick one:

  • Would I trust this company with my browsing list?
  • What does its no-logs policy actually cover (connection times, IP addresses, DNS)?
  • Has an independent party audited that claim? A policy is a promise, not a measurement.

4. A VPN does not make you anonymous to the website

The website sees the VPN's address, not yours. That does not stop it recognising you by what you give it: a logged-in Google account, cookies and site logins all travel inside the tunnel. If you are signed in, it still knows it is you.

5. At work, a VPN is a door, not a disguise

A company keeps internal systems on a private network the internet cannot reach. A VPN gateway at the edge faces both sides: it checks who you are (a password, often plus a code on your phone) and, if you are allowed, opens the outer envelope and drops the original inside the private network. IPsec (RFC 4301, section 3.1) covers exactly this case, traffic "between a security gateway and a host". Early VPN protocols were mainly built for business remote access (PPTP, 1996; secondary sources), which is why the same envelope trick does two different jobs.

Remember it like this

HTTPS seals the letter. A VPN hides the address from your network and shows it to the VPN provider.

Sources: RFC 9076 s5.1, RFC 8484 s8.1, RFC 6066 s3, RFC 9849 intro and s3.2, RFC 4301 s3.1 and s4.1, Google Chrome Help "Use secure DNS", Mozilla Support "Configure DNS over HTTPS protection levels in Firefox". Checked 2026-11-19.
