You commented EDGE — this is the one-pager.

Gradient descent trains right on the line where it should explode.

In a bowl-shaped loss valley, each gradient step multiplies your distance from the minimum by 1 minus your learning rate times the valley's steepness. Push that product past 2 and the multiplier drops below −1: every step overshoots further than the last, and the run explodes. Full-batch gradient descent — the whole training set, every step — does not sit safely under that line. It makes the valley steeper on its own until the steepness reaches it, then hovers a hair above, the loss jumping step to step but still falling over the long run. The paper that found this called it the edge of stability.

Nº001 · The speed limit

Two over the learning rate is where every hop starts landing higher

Near the bottom of a bowl-shaped valley, gradient descent's error from one step to the next obeys one rule: multiply the previous error by 1 − η × a, where η is the learning rate and a is the steepness of the valley's steepest wall (its curvature's top eigenvalue). Stay under η × a = 2 and each hop lands closer to the floor than the last. Land exactly on it and hops bounce at constant height, forever. Go past it and the multiplier's size passes 1, so every hop lands further out than the last, until the step leaves the valley.

A multiplier whose size is bigger than 1 means each hop lands further from the floor than the last one did, not just on the opposite wall: the hop sizes form a geometric sequence with a ratio bigger than 1, which has no ceiling. That is why, in a fixed bowl, η × a > 2 does not just make training worse — it makes it diverge. The paper states the case for a single quadratic direction directly: “If a>2/η, then (1−ηa)<−1, so the sequence will diverge.”

Nº002 · Progressive sharpening

Nobody touches the learning rate — the valley steepens on its own

The learning rate here is fixed, chosen once and never adjusted mid-run. What moves is the valley itself. While its steepest wall stays safely under the 2/η line, gradient descent has an active bias toward making that wall steeper, step after step, run after run — not because anything went wrong, but because that is what plain gradient descent does by default on the way to the edge. The paper calls this progressive sharpening.

“So long as the sharpness is small enough for gradient descent to be stable (<2/η), gradient descent has an overwhelming tendency to continually increase the sharpness.”

Cohen, Kaur, Li, Kolter & Talwalkar, 2021, arXiv 2103.00065

Nº003 · The edge of stability

The steepness stalls just above the line, and the loss keeps falling anyway

Once the valley's steepest wall reaches the 2/η line, it stops climbing and hovers there instead of crossing it. The loss trace does not fall smoothly while that happens: it jumps step to step, yet its long-run slope still points down. A fixed bowl at that same steepness would already have flown out of the valley (Nº001); a real network's loss surface keeps reshaping itself under the ball instead, and holds. Why it holds there is a question the paper leaves open — no mechanism for the survival is claimed here or in the film.

“The sharpness hovers right at, or just above, the value 2/η; and (2) the train loss behaves non-monotonically, yet consistently decreases over long timescales.”

Cohen, Kaur, Li, Kolter & Talwalkar, 2021, arXiv 2103.00065

Nº004 · Scope: what this is and is not

Full-batch gradient descent only — the paper says so itself

Every claim on this page is about full-batch gradient descent: one gradient step computed over the entire training set, learning rate fixed. The authors are explicit that it does not carry over to the stochastic version almost everyone actually trains with:

“Our precise characterization of the behavior of the sharpness only applies to full-batch gradient descent.”

Cohen, Kaur, Li, Kolter & Talwalkar, 2021, arXiv 2103.00065, Section 6

For SGD they found something closer to the opposite:

“In contrast, during SGD, the sharpness does not always settle at any fixed value, let alone one that can be numerically predicted from the hyperparameters.”

Cohen, Kaur, Li, Kolter & Talwalkar, 2021, arXiv 2103.00065, Section 6

Nothing on this page or in the film is a claim about SGD, minibatches, Adam or any other adaptive optimiser; about ChatGPT or any other LLM's training run; about a share of training time spent at the edge; or about a speed-up from training there. None of that is in the paper's full-batch results, and none of it is said here.
