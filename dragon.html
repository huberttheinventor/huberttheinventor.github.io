DRAGON / THE FOLD RULE CARD

The fold rule card: the dragon curve

Fold a strip of paper in half, the same way, again and again. Open it and set every crease to a right angle (a square corner, 90 degrees). Look down at its edge: the zigzag is a dragon curve. One small rule, repeated, builds the whole shape.

The fold rule

  1. Take a long, thin strip of paper.
  2. Fold it in half, right end over onto the left end. (Folding the other way works too: it draws the mirror image.)
  3. Fold the folded strip in half again, the same way. Repeat until you have folded n times.
  4. Unfold it, and open every crease to a right angle.

After 5 folds the paper is 32 layers thick, so 4 or 5 folds is a good place to stop by hand. The code below goes further.

Count it: n folds give 2ⁿ pieces and 2ⁿ − 1 creases

Every fold doubles the layers, and each layer becomes one piece when you unfold. The pieces sit in a row with one crease between each pair of neighbours, so there is always one crease fewer than pieces.

The crease list, and how to read it off your strip

Walk along the opened strip from one end. At each crease, write R if the strip turns right and L if it turns left.

foldscrease list
1 foldR
2 foldsRRL
3 foldsRRLRRLL
4 foldsRRLRRLL R RRLLRLL

The rule for the next list: take the old list, add a new R in the middle, then add the old list again, backwards, with every R and L swapped. The 4-fold row is spaced to show the three parts. Written as 1s and 0s (R = 1, L = 0), the 5-fold list starts 1101100111001001: mathematicians call it the regular paperfolding sequence.

Your strip may not read exactly this list. Start from the other end, or turn the strip over, and you get it backwards, or with R and L swapped, or both. Every one of those readings draws the same dragon or its mirror image.

For coders: crease number k (counting from 1) can be read directly. Halve k until it is odd. If that odd number leaves remainder 1 when divided by 4, the crease is R; remainder 3, it is L.

Why it is recursive: dragon(n) = dragon(n−1) + turn90(dragon(n−1))

Recursive means the rule uses itself on a smaller version. In the heading, turn90 means "the same shape, turned a quarter turn". The dragon after n folds is the dragon after n − 1 folds, followed by a copy of it, pinned at its end point and swung a quarter turn (90 degrees). The copy is walked from its far end back, which is why the list's second half is backwards with R and L swapped. With the lists above, starting off to the right as in the picture, the swing is anticlockwise.

So each half of the dragon is a smaller dragon. Measure the straight line from a half's start to its end: it is 0.7071 (1 ÷ √2) times as long as the whole dragon's, and tilted 45 degrees. That is why every part looks like the whole.

Corners touch, lines never cross

Draw the dragon on squared paper, one square per piece. From 4 folds on, the path comes back to a grid point it has visited before, and two corners touch there (1 touch point at 4 folds, 335 at 10, 28,555 at 16). But:

  • no grid line is ever used twice, so no piece lies on top of another;
  • no point is visited more than twice;
  • the path turns at every crease, so when it comes back to a point it turns a corner there instead of going straight through. The two visits use two different corners of that point.

Round the corners a little and the two visits pull apart. That is what "never crosses itself" means here: corners touch, lines never cross. Our check confirms all three points for every fold count from 1 to 16 (up to 65,536 pieces). MathWorld says its "right angle approximations" (the square-cornered drawings at each fold count) "can touch themselves at vertices" (corner points).

Four dragons and the endless grid

Start four dragons at one point, heading right, up, left and down. At 16 folds each (262,144 pieces in all), no two pieces share a grid line, nothing crosses, and together they use every grid line inside a square around the start:

8 folds each−5 to 5

The square keeps growing, but at any fixed number of folds the grid further out has gaps. Only in the limit (what the shapes approach if you could fold forever) do the four dragons cover the whole endless grid. Indiana University's maths gallery: "The four dragon curves never cross themselves or each other" and, "when extended forever, the dragon curves would pass through every integer segment in the plane exactly once" (an integer segment is one grid line between neighbouring grid points). It credits the proof to Chandler Davis and Donald Knuth (1970). We did not read their paper, only that summary.

For the curious: a finite area, an endless edge

Shrink each dragon so its two ends are 1 apart. Thicken each piece into a small diamond (a square standing on its corner, with the piece as its diagonal). Diamonds on different grid lines never overlap, and no line is used twice, so the area is exactly 1/2 at every fold count we checked (1 to 16). Wikipedia gives the same for the finished dragon: "its area is 1/2".

The edge of that diamond shape keeps growing: 10.87 at 8 folds, 15.69 at 10 and 46.55 at 16, about 1.2 times longer with each fold (1.197 to 1.202 times, from 8 to 16 folds). Wikipedia says the edge of the finished dragon "has infinite length". It also gives that edge a fractal dimension of 2 log₂ λ ≈ 1.5236, where λ ≈ 1.6956 solves λ³ − λ² − 2 = 0. A fractal dimension is a number between 1 (a smooth line) and 2 (a filled-in area) that measures how crinkly the edge is.

Run it yourself (Python, nothing to install)

import turtle

def creases(n):                   # the crease list after n folds: R = right turn, L = left turn
    s = ""
    for _ in range(n):            # old list, a new middle R, then the old list backwards with R and L swapped
        s = s + "R" + s[::-1].translate(str.maketrans("RL", "LR"))
    return s

for n in range(1, 5):
    print(n, "folds:", creases(n), "->", len(creases(n)) + 1, "pieces")

n, step, r = 10, 7, 2             # folds, piece length and corner rounding, in pixels
t = turtle.Turtle()
t.hideturtle()
turtle.tracer(0)                  # draw in one go; delete this line to watch it draw (slowly)
t.forward(r)
for c in creases(n) + ".":        # "." marks the end of the strip
    t.forward(step - 2 * r)
    if c == "R": t.circle(-r, 90)    # a rounded right turn, so touching corners stay apart
    elif c == "L": t.circle(r, 90)   # a rounded left turn
    else: t.forward(r)
turtle.update()
turtle.done()

It prints:

1 folds: R -> 2 pieces
2 folds: RRL -> 4 pieces
3 folds: RRLRRLL -> 8 pieces
4 folds: RRLRRLLRRRLLRLL -> 16 pieces

Then a window opens with the dragon after 10 folds (1,024 pieces). It uses Python's built-in turtle module. The corners are rounded, so where two corners touch they stay about 1.8 pixels apart instead of looking like a crossing. Change n to see other fold counts.

Honest limits

  1. Paper is not perfect. You can only open creases to roughly 90 degrees, and thickness doubles with every fold.
  2. "Never crosses" does not mean "never touches". Corners do touch. Drawn with sharp corners, a touch can look like a crossing; rounding the corners shows it is not.
  3. Filling the grid needs forever. At any fixed number of folds, four dragons cover a square around the start, not the whole grid.
  4. Our checks are finite. We checked up to 16 folds. The "forever" statements are quoted from the sources below.
  5. Two kinds of area. The 1/2 for every fold count is our diamond measure; the 1/2 and the endless edge of the finished dragon are Wikipedia's.
