Open weight is not open source.

DeepSeek ships its models under the MIT licence, and MIT is an open source licence. The model itself is not open source. A licence only covers what a company hands over, and what most of them hand over is one thing: the weights, the block of numbers a model settles on during training. That is the brain. Real open source also hands over the blueprint: the training code, the architecture, and enough about the data to rebuild it. Most releases give you the brain and let the licence imply the blueprint.

A licence covers what they gave you, not what they kept.

DeepSeek ships its models under the MIT licence, and MIT is genuinely an open source licence. But a licence only covers what a company hands you, and what most of them hand you is one thing: the weights, the block of numbers a model settles on during training. Weights are the brain. The licence just made it sound like the blueprint as well.

Open weight still buys you the brain.

Run it on your own machines and stop calling anyone else's server. Fine-tune it, shrink it to fit on a laptop, lock it inside your own building. Nobody can switch it off on you. A brain, on its own, is a lot; it just is not the whole system.

What stays behind the weights.

The data the model read, the pipeline that trained it, and the code that cleaned that data first: none of it ships with an open weight release. It is the cake without the recipe. Qwen, to its credit, calls its own releases open weight rather than open source, which is the honest word for DeepSeek, Qwen, and GLM alike. Read the licence too: Llama's has a number in it, 700 million monthly users, and past that you ask Meta for permission. Meta can say no. That is not what open has ever meant.

Count what is on the table.

Real open source puts four things on the table: the weights, the training code, the architecture, and enough about the data to rebuild the model. Rebuilding it still takes millions of dollars of computing power, but the drawings are there, and that is the difference. Even the referees disagree on how much of that fourth item counts: the Open Source Initiative's 2024 definition does not demand the data itself, only a description good enough to rebuild from, and not everyone accepted that. So when a company says open source, do not look for a download button. Count what is on the table. Fewer than four things is open weight, whatever the release calls itself.
