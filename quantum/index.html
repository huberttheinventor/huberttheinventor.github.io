Nothing lands in the middle.

Prepare an electron. Measure its spin along one fixed direction. Record the answer. Repeat. The dots collect on two levels. You can change the odds — but nothing fills the middle.

The whole mental model, one picture. Different odds. Same possible answers.

Take it with you: the quantum schematic, with the dot experiment and its equation on one sheet.

A dot is a record, not a particle.

Each dot represents a separate preparation and measurement. It is not an electron travelling across the screen, and the two rows are not places where particles sit. They are the two possible readings for this spin measurement: +ℏ/2 and −ℏ/2.

Keep the measurement direction fixed. Preparing the electron differently changes how likely each reading is. It does not create a third reading halfway between them, and it does not rewrite dots already recorded.

Now make one answer certain.

Choose “Certain upper” in the diagram. For this ideal measurement, the special upper-result state gives +ℏ/2 every time. The certainty belongs to the preparation and the chosen measurement together.

That special state is an eigenstate. Its certain reading is the corresponding eigenvalue. The names describe the relationship you just saw.

The maths names the possible answer.

The equation connects the measurement rule, the special state and its reading. It does not mean that measuring physically multiplies an electron, or that every preparation already has a certain answer.

Return to another preparation in the diagram: the odds change, while the two possible readings remain the same.

What this picture does not prove.

This is an ideal spin-½ example along one fixed axis. Other observables can have more than two possible outcomes, or a continuous range. The two-row record alone does not establish that the preparation was a superposition.
