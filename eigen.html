Two directions the wind cannot turn.

A matrix usually rotates a vector as well as stretching it. Pick the right direction, though, and the rotation disappears: the matrix only stretches. Those two directions carry the whole solution to x' = Ax, and every other starting point is just a mixture of them.

The whole mental model, one picture. Everything after it is annotation.

Take it with you: the five-step schematic, the same method worked end to end on one page.

Nº001

Infinitely many solutions, and two are enough to build them all.

x' = Ax is a first order linear system: at every point it names the direction and speed the state is currently moving in. Fix a starting point and the whole path is determined, so there is one solution per starting point, and the plane is full of starting points. That is where the infinity comes from. It is not a flaw in the equation, it is a description of every history the system could have had.

The equation is also linear, and linear means solutions add. If x₁(t) and x₂(t) both solve x' = Ax, so does any combination c₁x₁(t) + c₂x₂(t). In two dimensions, two solutions that are not parallel are enough to reach every starting point by choosing the right c₁ and c₂, the same way two non parallel vectors reach every point in a plane. Find two honest solutions and the constants do the rest.

Nº002

Along one direction, the matrix stops turning.

Multiply a generic vector by A and it comes out pointing somewhere else, rotated as well as scaled. Pick v = (1, 1) instead and Av = (1.5, 1.5), the same vector times 1.5. Pick v = (1, −1) and Av = (−0.5, 0.5), the same vector times −0.5. Along these two lines, and only these two, A behaves like a single number instead of a matrix: it stretches or shrinks the vector and sends it back along the same line it started on.

That number is the eigenvalue λ, and the direction it belongs to is the eigenvector v. Once a solution is confined to a line where multiplying by A is the same as multiplying by λ, the whole vector equation x' = Ax collapses to the scalar equation x' = λx, and that equation has one family of solutions: x(t) = ce^{λt}. A positive λ grows, a negative λ decays, and there is nothing else it can do on a straight line through the origin.

Nº003

Two straight lines, one saddle, and a sign worth watching.

The map has exactly two straight-line solutions, one along each eigenvector. Start exactly on (1, 1) and the state moves straight out along it forever, scaled by e^{1.5t}. Start exactly on (1, −1) and it moves straight in toward the origin, scaled by e^{−0.5t}. Every other starting point is the sum c₁e^{1.5t}(1, 1) + c₂e^{−0.5t}(1, −1), which is why the curved trajectories on the map hug the inward line early and swing onto the outward line late: the shrinking term stops mattering and the growing term takes over.

The signs of the two eigenvalues tell you the shape before you compute a single trajectory. One positive and one negative, as here, means a saddle: attracting on one axis, repelling on the other, and almost every path eventually leaves. Two positive eigenvalues would pull everything outward, a source. Two negative would pull everything in, a sink. The picture is a direct readout of two numbers.
