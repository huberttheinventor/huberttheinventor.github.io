PLATE / THE SPINNING PLATE CARD

The spinning plate card

The Fourier transform in one picture: let a jet engine's shaking drip onto a spinning plate, and the plate only tips at the speeds that match the notes inside the shaking. Try every speed, plot how far it tips, and you have drawn the Fourier transform. When a bearing starts wearing out, it adds a new note, so a new spike rises where the plot was flat.

The film calls the balance point the Fourier transform. Strictly, the transform is the ink's total, and the balance point is that total divided by the record length (or by the number of drops). Dividing shrinks every spike by the same amount and moves none of them, so the picture stays the same. We treat the pen as resting in the middle before the record starts and after it ends.

All engine numbers on this card come from a made-up (schematic) engine signal, labelled illustrative. No real engine speed is given: the 50 turns per second below is made up.

The picture, in plain words

  1. A sensor on the engine's casing feels it shake. That shaking (the film calls it the hum) is a handful of notes from the spinning shafts, added together into one wiggly line.
  2. The line swings a pen across a round plate that sits on a pin. The pen follows how the casing shakes: to one side of the middle, then to the other. When the casing is still, the pen rests in the middle.
  3. The plate spins at a speed you choose (turns per second). At a steady beat the pen drips one drop of ink, always the same size. Each drop stays where it lands while the plate keeps turning under the pen.
  4. After the whole record, find the plate's balance point: the plain average of every drop's spot.
  5. How far that balance point sits from the pin is how far the plate tips.

Why only the notes tip the plate

A note is a steady wiggle: out to one side, back through the middle, out to the other side, over and over.

  • Plate speed matches none of the notes. The out-swings and back-swings find the plate facing different ways, so ink on one side gets matched by ink as far out on the other. They cancel, and the balance point stays at the pin.
  • Plate spins exactly as fast as the front fan. Each time the fan's note pushes the pen out, the plate faces the same way. Each time it pulls the pen back, the plate's turned half way round, so that ink lands on the same side. The fan's ink piles up there and the plate tips, while the other notes still cancel. For a note with swing size A, the balance point sits A ÷ 2 from the pin (why half: see "For the curious" below).
  • The engine's shaking is notes added together. Each note tips the plate only at its own speed (and its mirror speed), so one sweep of the dial finds them all. A note twice as big tips twice as far.

The engine, healthy and worn (illustrative)

The schematic engine has three usual notes: the fan (one wiggle per fan turn, size 1), double the fan's speed (size 0.5) and the core shaft, a second shaft turning at its own speed, 1.37 × the fan's (size 0.8). The worn bearing adds a faint knock at 3.58 × the fan's speed (size 0.1, about 0.53% of the signal's power). Record: 1 second, fan speed 50 turns per second (illustrative), 4,000 drops per second. The healthy plot has spikes at fan, fan × 2 and core, and is flat between 3 × and 4.2 × the fan. With the worn bearing, a small new spike (0.050) stands on that flat stretch, more than six times taller than anything else in that stretch. The usual spikes do not change.

How to read the plot. A spike is a speed where the shaking keeps landing on one side of the plate. A new spike means something new is shaking.

Why 3.58: a bearing's knock speed is set by its shape and the shaft speed, and is not a whole multiple of the shaft's speed. For the outer ring, BPFO = (n × shaft speed ÷ 2) × (1 − (d ÷ D) × cos φ), with n balls of diameter d on a pitch circle of diameter D at contact angle φ. An example industrial ball bearing (9 balls, 7.94 mm ball, 39.04 mm pitch circle, contact angle 0) gives 4.5 × (1 − 0.2034) = 3.58 × shaft speed. That bearing is not an aircraft part; the ratio only places the schematic spike at a non-whole multiple.

A small example you can check by hand

Eight drops in a record, one per beat. Two notes: one that wiggles once per record with swing 1, and one that wiggles three times per record with swing 0.5. The pen heights are

g = 1.5, 0.3536, 0, −0.3536, −1.5, −0.3536, 0, 0.3536     (average 0.0000, so the pen rests in the middle)

Try plates turning 0 to 7 times per record (k turns per record; here time is counted in beats):

Speed 1 tips 0.50 (swing 1 ÷ 2) and speed 3 tips 0.25 (swing 0.5 ÷ 2). Every other whole speed stays level. Speeds 5 and 7 are the same two notes seen from a plate spinning the other way (a real signal looks the same in the mirror), so you only need the first half. Start the record one beat later and the distances stay 0.50 and 0.25, but the direction changes (at speed 1 from 0 degrees to 45 degrees); the usual plot throws that direction away.

For the curious: the formula

    balance point at speed f  =  (1 / T) × ∫ g(t) · e^(−2πi·f·t) dt      (the Fourier transform at f, divided by T)
    with N drops              =  (1 / N) × Σ g(tₙ) · e^(−2πi·f·tₙ)
symbolin plain words
g(t)how far (and to which side) the shaking swings the pen at time t; 0 is the middle
t, Ta moment in seconds; T is the length of the whole record
fhow fast the plate spins, in turns per second (Hz)
e^(−2πi·f·t)"how far the plate has turned by time t": a marker that goes once round a circle for every turn of the plate (the minus sign says the plate turns under the pen, so seen from the plate each drop is carried backwards)
imarks the second direction on the plate, at right angles to the first, so one number can hold both "how far across" and "how far up"
∫ ... dt, Σadd up the drops (the integral is the same sum with the drops made tiny and endless)
1/T, 1/Ndivide by the record length or the number of drops, which turns the total into the average
N, tₙN is the number of drops (record length × drops per second); tₙ is the moment of drop number n

The answer is a pair of numbers: how far the balance point is from the pin, and which way (its direction, called the phase). A spike plot normally keeps only how far.

Why a note of swing A tips the plate only A ÷ 2: a steady wiggle is two half-size circles turning opposite ways. The plate catches one of them, and the other shows up at the mirror speed (the plate spinning the other way, as speeds 5 and 7 show in the small example).

Honest limits

  1. Vibration, not sound. The "hum" is the casing's shaking, felt by a sensor on the engine (an accelerometer, or a laser in the 2025 study below), not sound picked up by a microphone.
  2. The usual notes are shaft speeds and their multiples. A real engine's predictable shaking sits at each shaft's speed, multiples of it and some specific non-whole combinations. "A handful of notes" simplifies that; the plot shows a schematic three (fan, fan × 2, core).
  3. A new spike is not always alone. A bearing's knock speed is set by its geometry and the shaft speeds, at a non-whole multiple of shaft speed. But other sources can sit at the same speed, and the highest peak is not always the bearing's, so real systems first take out the predictable notes. A new spike says "look here", not "this bearing, for certain".
  4. The record length sets how sharp the spikes are. With the 1 second record, the bearing spike is about 1.2 turns per second wide at half its height. Cut the record to 0.1 s (only 5 turns of the fan) and the bearing's spike spreads into a bump about nine to ten times wider (9.5 × measured). Its peak moves (0.074 at 183.5 turns per second, against 0.041 at the bearing speed), and the usual notes leak into the flat stretch (up to 0.057 in the healthy engine). You can no longer read off which speed it is, and one plot no longer shows a clean new spike on a flat stretch. The longer you record, the sharper the spikes. A short record gives better timing and blurrier speeds.
  5. "Starts wearing out" means an early, measurable defect, not a failure date. A worn bearing first gets small pits on its rings and rollers (spalling). This card gives no lead time ("weeks before it fails"); no aircraft-engine source we read gives one.
  6. Subtract the average first. If the pen rests off the middle (a sensor with an offset), the plate tips at speed 0 by that offset, whatever the notes are. Run x = x - x.mean() before anything else.
  7. The plot drops the direction. It shows how far the plate tips, not which way. The direction says when each note started, and you need it to rebuild the signal from the numbers.
  8. The picture is the maths, not a real plate. A physical plate on a pin would also wobble. This plate does only the averaging. The engine is idealised too: three usual notes plus the bearing's, clean, lasting the whole record, with no noise.
  9. The drops must come fast enough. A note faster than half the drops per second looks like a slower one (aliasing). Here 4,000 drops per second is plenty for notes up to 179 turns per second.
  10. Turns per record, not per second. "k turns per record" is k ÷ T turns per second. An FFT only tries whole numbers
of turns per record, so a note that does not fit a whole number of turns spreads over neighbouring speeds. In the
code below the core note (68.5 turns per second) splits into two FFT spikes of about 0.25, at 68 and 69.

What NumPy's FFT is

np.fft.fft(x) adds up the drops for a whole row of speeds, 0, 1, 2, 3 ... turns per record, in one fast go. It returns one complex number per speed: the balance point's sum (how far and which way). Divide by the number of drops N to get the average, and take abs(...) to keep only how far. That list of distances is the spike plot.

Run it yourself (NumPy)

import numpy as np

fs = 4000                                    # drops per second (one drop per tick)
t = np.arange(fs) / fs                       # a 1 second record
F = 50                                       # fan speed in turns per second: illustrative, not a real engine

def hum(bearing):                            # the sensor's line: fan, fan x2, core shaft, worn bearing's knock
    return (np.cos(2*np.pi*F*t) + 0.5*np.cos(2*np.pi*2*F*t) + 0.8*np.cos(2*np.pi*1.37*F*t)
            + bearing*np.cos(2*np.pi*3.58*F*t))

def tip(x, t, speed):                        # balance point of the ink at this plate speed
    return abs(np.mean(x * np.exp(-2j*np.pi*speed*t)))

healthy, worn = hum(0), hum(0.1)
for name, speed in (("between", 160), ("fan", F), ("fan x2", 2*F), ("core", 1.37*F), ("bearing", 3.58*F)):
    print(f"{name:8s} {speed:6.1f} turns/s   healthy {tip(healthy, t, speed):.3f}   worn {tip(worn, t, speed):.3f}")

H = abs(np.fft.fft(healthy)) / fs            # every whole speed at once (k turns per record)
W = abs(np.fft.fft(worn)) / fs
new = int(np.argmax((W - H)[:fs//2]))
print("biggest new FFT spike at", new, "turns/s:", round(H[new], 3), "->", round(W[new], 3))
print("core at 68.5 turns/s splits over FFT speeds 68 and 69:", W[68:70].round(3))

s = slice(0, 400)                            # a 0.1 second record: only 5 turns of the fan
near = max(tip(worn[s], t[s], f) for f in np.arange(150, 210, 0.5) if abs(f - 179) > 3)
print("0.1 s record: bearing speed tips", round(tip(worn[s], t[s], 179), 3),
      "but the smeared bump peaks elsewhere at", round(near, 3))

print("pen resting off-centre by 1: speed 0 tips", round(tip(worn + 1, t, 0), 3))
print("after subtracting the average:", round(tip((worn + 1) - (worn + 1).mean(), t, 0), 3))

Where this is used

In 2025, engineers measured F100-PW-229 fighter jet engines on a test bench with a laser vibrometer (a laser that reads how a surface shakes, without touching it) and processed the signals with the FFT. Their abstract reports frequency features "indicative of rotor imbalances, bearing wear, and structural anomalies". Prokopowicz, Ciupek, Maciąg, Gajewski, Sielicki, "Advanced Spectral Diagnostics of Jet Engine Vibrations Using Non-Contact Laser Vibrometry and Fourier Methods", Energies 18(18):4837, 2025, DOI 10.3390/en18184837 (https://doi.org/10.3390/en18184837). We read the abstract, not the full paper.
