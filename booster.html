How a booster comes home.

For most of the space age a rocket flew once. Reusable rockets bring the booster back: the bottom stage that does the hard pushing off the pad. About two and a half minutes in, it lets go of the top stage, high above the Earth and going thousands of kilometres an hour the wrong way. Six steps get it from there onto a pad or a drone ship, standing upright, with no pilot.

Nº001 · The six steps

What each step fixes

Falcon 9 first stage, after separation · sources below, read 2026-11-04
1 FlipIt is flying the wrong way, so small nitrogen gas thrusters flip it around.
2 Boostback burnOn flights with fuel to spare, the engines relight and push it back toward the landing site. Missions that need all their energy for the cargo skip it.
3 Entry burnIt drops into the air very fast, so the engines light again. This burn slows it down, so the air doesn't heat it as much.
4 Grid finsLower down, four criss-cross panels near the top bite into the air and do the steering.
5 Landing burnHovering wastes fuel every second, so it waits, then lights the engine for one last burn, timed to reach zero speed right at the ground.
6 LegsThe legs swing out, so it lands upright, on a pad or a drone ship far out at sea. A computer flies it: it checks where it is and how fast it's falling, then plans a new path in a fraction of a second.
The rocket has to hit zero velocity at exactly zero altitude. Lars Blackmore (SpaceX), Autonomous Precision Landing of Space Rockets, NAE Frontiers of Engineering

Nº002 · The two crashes

Where the steps broke in 2015

January 2015, the first try at landing on a ship — step 4. The booster made it back to the landing ship but crashed: the grid fins ran out of hydraulic fluid just before touchdown. SpaceX said the next booster would carry more fluid.

Grid fins worked extremely well from hypersonic velocity to subsonic, but ran out of hydraulic fluid right before landing. Elon Musk, as quoted by the Planetary Society, 12 Jan 2015

April 2015, the second try on the ship — step 5. A throttle valve reacted slower than expected. The booster reached the deck still sliding sideways, wavered and tipped over.

Cause of hard rocket landing confirmed as due to slower than expected throttle valve response. Elon Musk, as quoted by Spaceflight Now, 21 Apr 2015

The first booster landed intact on land on 21 December 2015 (Florida time), and the first landing on a drone ship came on 8 April 2016.

Nº003 · Why bring it back

The hardware is the expensive part

In December 2015 Musk put the whole rocket at about $60 million to build and the fuel for one flight at about $200,000 (Spaceflight Now, 22 Dec 2015). So the hardware is what you want back.

Reuse still costs something Parts wear out, checks take time, and fuel kept for the trip home is fuel that can't lift cargo. The first booster to fly a second time, in 2017, took several months to inspect, had some hardware replaced and was test-fired before it flew again (Spaceflight Now, 31 Mar 2017).

The honest scope

Every claim on this page and on the card was checked on 2026-11-04 against the press and paper pages listed below; nothing comes from memory. No SpaceX, NASA or FAA page was read for it: SpaceX and Musk are quoted as the press quoted them. The separation speed is given as “thousands of kilometres an hour” because it differs from mission to mission. That the entry burn means less heating is our reading of two sources (the burn slows the booster for re-entry; faster descents mean more heating), not a sentence from SpaceX. Whether one or three engines fire at landing depends on the mission, so the card says “the engine”, not a count.
