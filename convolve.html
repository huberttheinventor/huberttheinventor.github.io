CONVOLVE / THE FLIP AND SLIDE CARD

The flip and slide card

Convolution in one picture: to hear your voice in a room, flip the room's answer, slide it across your sound, multiply where they overlap, and add up the overlap. Each slide position gives one moment of what you would hear.

(f * g)(t)  =  ∫ f(τ) · g(t − τ) dτ
symbolin plain words
f(τ)your sound: how loud your voice was at moment τ
gthe room's answer to one sharp click (its "impulse response"): a loud start, then a fading tail
tthe moment you are listening at, "right now"
t − τhow long ago you said the bit at moment τ
g(t − τ)how loud that bit's echo still is, that long after you said it
∫ … dτadd up every bit of your voice, each one weighted by its echo

Why the flip: every bit of your voice sets off its own copy of the room's answer, and at any moment you hear all of those copies at once. A bit you said a moment ago is near the loud start of its echo; a bit you said long ago is out in the faded tail. Writing the room's answer backwards, g(t − τ), puts its loud start on "right now" and its faded tail on "long ago", so each bit of your voice is multiplied by exactly how loud its echo still is.

The four moves:

  1. Flip the room's answer so its loud start faces "now" and its tail points into the past.
  2. Slide it to the moment t you want to hear.
  3. Multiply your sound by the flipped answer wherever they overlap.
  4. Add up everything under that product. The total is one point of the output. Slide a little further and repeat.

Which one you flip does not matter: flipping your voice instead of the room gives the same result.

Two rooms, same slide (the two responses used in the film, measured 2026-10-23 with a Schroeder decay fit):

room's answerhow long its tail rings (T20)what the same slide gives
a cathedralabout 3.1 sa long, ringing echo
a bathroomabout 0.6 sa quick, tight echo

Honest caveats:

  • A hand clap is only a rough stand-in for a perfect click (no real sound is a perfect click; a brief pulse is a first approximation). Careful measurements often use a sine sweep instead. A study that measured an opera house with hand claps and a phone found its echo times agreed with published values only over part of the frequency range. A clap is still a cheap, workable way to hear a room.
  • The film draws smooth outlines so the slide is easy to see. The real calculation runs on every sample of the sound wave, which is what produced the cathedral and bathroom audio you heard.
  • Image and audio neural networks run the same slide, multiply and add, but their layers skip the flip (frameworks compute "cross-correlation"). Because the network learns its shape, it can simply learn it backwards, so nothing is lost.

Try it yourself (Python 3 with NumPy):

import numpy as np

voice = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.8, 0.0])        # f: a tiny dry "hey"
room  = np.array([1.0, 0.6, 0.35, 0.2, 0.1])       # g: the room's answer to one click

out = np.convolve(voice, room)                     # flip, slide, multiply, add up
print(np.round(out, 3))                            # [0. 1. 1.1 1.45 0.855 0.48 0.21 0.08 0.]

# one output dot by hand, at t = 3: add f[tau] * g[t - tau] over every tau
t = 3
dot = sum(voice[tau] * room[t - tau] for tau in range(len(voice)) if 0 <= t - tau < len(room))
print(round(dot, 3), round(out[t], 3))             # 1.45 1.45

# a neural-network layer slides WITHOUT the flip (cross-correlation);
# sliding the pre-flipped shape gives exactly the convolution back
print(np.allclose(np.correlate(voice, room[::-1], "full"), out))   # True
print(len(voice), len(room), len(out))             # 5 5 9: the output is longer than the voice
