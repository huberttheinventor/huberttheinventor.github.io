Shazam never hears your song. It hears the circles inside it.

Shazam has to name a song from a few seconds of noisy audio picked up in a loud bar, checked against a catalogue of over a million tracks. It is not listening for a tune. Let's design it the way the maths actually works: sound as spinning circles, the circles turned into a list by a Fourier transform, and a fingerprint built only from the loudest circles that survive the noise.

Five steps, one picture. Everything after it is annotation.

Take it with you: the Fourier schematic, the six steps that turn a few seconds of noisy audio into one matched song.

Circles, not a soundwave.

Every sound, including a song in a loud bar, can be built back up from a stack of spinning circles: some big and slow, some small and fast. Chain them tip to tip and the last circle in the chain traces the same wave a microphone would record. That stack, described as a list of how fast each circle spins and how big it is, is what mathematicians call a Fourier transform. It is not a Shazam invention; it is the standard way to describe any sound as frequencies instead of as air pressure over time.

The spectrogram.

Shazam does not take that list once. It takes it many times a second and lays the results side by side, time running one way across the image and faster circles sitting higher up. The result is a spectrogram: a picture of every frequency in the recording, moment by moment. The noise of a bar, voices, traffic, other music, smears across most of it. But the song's own loudest circles still poke through, like stars against a bright sky.

The fingerprint: a constellation.

Shazam keeps only those surviving peaks, the loudest points in the spectrogram, plotted across time. Avery Wang, the author of the 2003 paper on the method, calls the result a constellation map. The peaks are picked "due to their robustness in the presence of noise," and the paper reports the search still works "in the presence of foreground voices and other dominant noise." Shazam never stores the sound itself. It stores where the stars are.

The match, one diagonal line.

To find your song, Shazam lines your few seconds of constellation map up against the constellation map already stored for every track it knows, over a million of them by Wang's own count. A wrong song scatters. The right song does not: its matching points fall in register with each other, which shows up in a scatterplot as points sitting on one diagonal line. That line is the whole answer. Shazam did not need to hear your song the way you did. It only needed the pattern of its own stars to line up with one stored map, and no other.
