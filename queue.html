QUEUE / THE WAIT TIME CARD

The wait time card

The rule in one line: in a line that moves at a steady pace, your wait is about the people ahead of you ÷ the people served per minute.

wait  ≈  people ahead  ÷  people served per minute
Little's law:  L = λW    (people in line = people per minute × minutes each spends in line)

Where the rule comes from: Little's law. In a steady line, the average number of people in it (L) equals the rate they come through (λ, people per minute) times the average time each one spends in it (W). Turn it around and W = L ÷ λ: count the people, divide by the rate, and you have the time. In a steady line as many people leave per minute as join, so you can measure λ at the till.

How to measure the rate in one minute:

  1. Watch the till (or all the tills that serve your line) for one minute.
  2. Count how many people leave. That count is your people served per minute.
  3. Divide the people ahead of you by that count.

Quicker version: time one customer at the till. 20 seconds each means 60 ÷ 20 = 3 per minute.

The second row is the film's turn: 18 people at two tills clear in 3 minutes, a minute sooner than 12 people at one. Pick the line that drains fastest, even when it looks longer.

When it breaks: the rule is about averages in a line that keeps a steady pace. It gives the average wait, not a promise for one person. If the pace changes (a slow customer, a till closing, a rush arriving), the rate you counted is out of date: count again. The law itself does not care about the mix of fast and slow customers or the order they are served in, as long as the line stays steady over time.

The same law in an AI server: requests are the people. Requests in the server ≈ requests per second × seconds each request takes (an illustrative example: 20 requests per second × 2 seconds each ≈ 40 requests in the server at once). One paper uses exactly this: Kong et al., "An Interpretable Latency Model for Speculative Decoding in LLM Serving" (arXiv 2605.15051, May 2026), infers effective batch size from request rate using Little's Law.

Try it yourself (Python 3, no libraries):

def wait_minutes(people_ahead, served_per_minute):
    return people_ahead / served_per_minute

for ahead, tills, per_till in [(12, 1, 3), (18, 2, 3), (12, 1, 1)]:
    print(f"{ahead:>2} ahead, {tills} till(s) at {per_till}/min: "
          f"{wait_minutes(ahead, tills * per_till):.1f} min")

# server form, L = rate x time each
print(f"in the server: {20 * 2} requests at once (20/s x 2 s)")
