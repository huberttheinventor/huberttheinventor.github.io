HORIZON / THE FORECAST HORIZON CARD

The forecast horizon card: why a better start buys so little extra time

Companion to Hubert film 17 (differential equations, part 2: two pendulums, one hair apart). Written 2026-10-20. The weather figures are for midlatitude day-to-day weather and for deterministic forecast skill.

The rule in one line

When a starting error keeps doubling, every ten times better start buys the same few extra doublings, so the time you can predict grows only with the logarithm of your precision.

T = (1/λ) · ln(S / ε)      ε = starting gap, S = gap that ruins the prediction, λ = growth rate
divide ε by 10  →  T grows by ln 10 / λ  =  3.3 doublings, every time

The double pendulum

Big swings only: at small swings it moves as two simple swings mixed and the gap stays small.

  • Measured growth rate λ = 7.5 ± 1.5 per second (Shinbrot et al. 1992, Am. J. Phys. 60:491: "a growth of uncertainties which is exponential with exponent λ=7.5±1.5 s−1").
  • So the gap doubles every ln 2 / 7.5 ≈ 0.09 s, and a ten times better start buys ln 10 / 7.5 ≈ 0.3 s.
  • Chaos needs large angles (Levien & Tan 1993, Am. J. Phys. 61:1038: "For large angle swings sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the signature of chaos, may be demonstrated").

The weather

  • "a skillful forecast lead time of midlatitude instantaneous weather is around 10 days"; "this number was 7 days 30 years ago" (Zhang et al. 2019, J. Atmos. Sci. 76:1077, read 2026-10-19).
  • "Reducing the current-day initial-condition uncertainty by an order of magnitude extends the deterministic forecast lead times of day-to-day weather by up to 5 days" (same paper).
  • "the ultimate limit of midlatitude day-to-day weather predictability is about 2 weeks" (same paper).
  • Forecasters measure the spread directly: ECMWF's ensemble is "51 forecasts", 50 with "slightly altered initial conditions", "up to 15 days ahead" (ecmwf.int, medium-range forecasts, read 2026-10-19).

Caveat: weather errors grow at different rates at different scales, so the 5 days is measured in forecast models, not computed from the formula above.

Try it yourself

Python 3, no libraries. Two pendulums released at 120 degrees, the upper arm nudged by 1e-9, 1e-10 and 1e-11 radians.

from math import sin, cos, pi
g = 9.81  # two 1 m arms, two equal bobs

def f(s):
    a, b, wa, wb = s; d = a - b; n = 3 - cos(2 * d)
    return (wa, wb,
            (-g * (3 * sin(a) + sin(a - 2 * b)) - 2 * sin(d) * (wb**2 + wa**2 * cos(d))) / n,
            2 * sin(d) * (2 * wa**2 + 2 * g * cos(a) + wb**2 * cos(d)) / n)

def rk4(s, h):
    k1 = f(s); k2 = f([x + h/2*k for x, k in zip(s, k1)])
    k3 = f([x + h/2*k for x, k in zip(s, k2)]); k4 = f([x + h*k for x, k in zip(s, k3)])
    return [x + h/6*(p + 2*q + 2*r + w) for x, p, q, r, w in zip(s, k1, k2, k3, k4)]

for eps in (1e-9, 1e-10, 1e-11):
    A, B, t = [2*pi/3, 2*pi/3, 0, 0], [2*pi/3 + eps, 2*pi/3, 0, 0], 0
    while abs(A[0] - B[0]) < 0.1:
        A, B, t = rk4(A, 0.0005), rk4(B, 0.0005), t + 0.0005
    print(f"start gap {eps:.0e} rad: the arms differ by 0.1 rad after {t:.2f} s")

Our run (Python 3.12.10, 2026-10-19): 14.23 s, 15.04 s, 17.15 s. A thousand times better start bought under three seconds. The extra time per tenfold is uneven (0.8 s, then 2.1 s) because the growth rate changes along the path; halving the step size gave 14.23, 15.04 and 17.12 s. This pendulum grows more slowly than Shinbrot's: going from 1e-9 to 0.1 rad is 26.6 doublings in 14.23 s, about one every half second. The rate depends on the pendulum and how high it starts; the rule does not.

The story

Around January 1961 Edward Lorenz restarted a weather simulation from a printout showing .506 for a stored .506127, and "all resemblance between the two had disappeared" (APS News, 2003).
